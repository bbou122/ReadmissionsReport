The business problem

Every 30-day bounce-back costs the hospital twice

Under CMS's Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program, hospitals with excess 30-day readmissions face direct Medicare reimbursement penalties — on top of the readmission's own cost, estimated at roughly $15,200 per event across published CMS/HCUP cost studies. The care coordination team's question was concrete: at the moment a patient is discharged, who is actually at risk of coming back, and which of the handful of things we control — follow-up scheduling, discharge paperwork, disposition — would move that risk if we fixed them?

This report walks through the full analysis: how the data was cleaned, which factors were tested, what the adjusted model says once confounders are controlled for, a simple risk-tiering rule a coordination team could actually use at the nurses' station, and a dollar estimate of the opportunity.

Key findings

Five things the data says clearly

01

A scheduled follow-up is the strongest lever the hospital actually controls

Patients discharged without a 7-day follow-up appointment are readmitted at 18.1%, vs. 13.0% for those with one scheduled — a gap that survives adjustment for age, comorbidities, and prior utilization (adjusted OR 0.65, 95% CI 0.57–0.74, p<0.001).

02

Recent utilization dwarfs every other predictor

Each additional hospital admission in the trailing 12 months raises the adjusted odds of readmission by ~56%; each additional ER visit by ~25%. Patients with 3+ prior admissions are readmitted at nearly 3× the rate of first-time patients.

03

Comorbidity burden compounds, it doesn't just add

Readmission climbs from 12.7% (no chronic conditions) to 23.6% (3+ of diabetes, heart failure, COPD, chronic kidney disease) — consistent with a patient whose overall physiologic reserve, not any single diagnosis, is what's driving risk.

04

A 5-input rule-based score cleanly stratifies risk

Built from the model's strongest, most significant predictors, the score separates a 10.0%-risk "Low" tier from a 40.4%-risk "Very High" tier using only information available at the moment of discharge — no model API required.

05

Closing the follow-up gap is worth an estimated $2.4M/year

45% of this cohort was discharged with no follow-up scheduled. Bringing their readmission rate to the "follow-up scheduled" benchmark would prevent an estimated 158 readmissions a year — against a total estimated annual readmission cost of $16.3M.

Finding in detail — follow-up scheduling

The single biggest controllable lever

Follow-up scheduling is the rare risk factor that is both statistically significant and directly actionable — a hospital can't undo a patient's admission history, but it can staff a discharge-planning workflow that books the 7-day follow-up before the patient leaves the building.

Why this is the headline recommendation

Unlike age or prior admissions, follow-up scheduling is a process metric, not a patient trait — it's the one variable in this analysis a hospital can change next quarter without changing who walks through the door.

Application — risk stratification

Turning the model into something a nurse can use at 2am

Regression coefficients aren't usable at the bedside. So the strongest, most significant predictors were converted into a simple point-based score (prior admissions, prior ER visits, comorbidity count, length of stay, age, discharge disposition, and whether a follow-up was scheduled) — the kind of checklist a discharge planner could actually fill out. Business impact

What closing the follow-up gap is worth

Applying a published mid-range per-readmission cost (~$15,200, CMS/HCUP range) to this cohort's 1,070 readmissions puts total estimated annual readmission cost at roughly $16.3M. If the ~45% of patients currently discharged without a follow-up were brought down to the readmission rate observed among patients who do get one scheduled, that's an estimated 158 fewer readmissions a year.

Recommendation

Prioritize scheduling 7-day follow-ups for patients who screen High or Very High on the risk score above (1,073 patients in this cohort, 27% of readmissions) rather than trying to close the gap for every discharge at once — it concentrates a fixed coordination-staff budget on the patients where it moves the most risk.

Methodology

How the numbers were produced

Data cleaning

Dropped 15 duplicate patient records, flagged 8 impossible negative length-of-stay values as missing, median-imputed 218 missing values across two columns, enforced types, and ran sanity-bound assertions. Every step is logged to cleaning_log.txt for a full audit trail.

Univariate testing

χ² tests of independence between each categorical factor and 30-day readmission — a first pass to confirm which factors were even worth putting in a model (follow-up scheduling, prior utilization, and age group all cleared p<0.001; insurance type did not).

Multivariable logistic regression

Fit by Newton-Raphson maximum likelihood, implemented directly with NumPy/SciPy. Reports each factor's adjusted odds ratio — its effect holding the other 16 factors constant — with 95% Wald confidence intervals. Out-of-sample discrimination checked on a held-out 25% split (AUC = 0.681, in the realistic 0.65–0.75 range typical of administrative readmission models).

Risk scoring

A transparent point-based rule built from the regression's strongest predictors, validated by confirming a clean, monotonic readmission-rate gradient across four tiers.

Cost modeling

Applied a published per-readmission cost estimate to translate the follow-up-scheduling finding into an annualized dollar figure.

Limitations — stated plainly

What this analysis can't claim

A credible analysis says what it can't show as clearly as what it can. Four caveats matter here:

Synthetic data

The public dataset this project targeted (UCI's Diabetes 130-US Hospitals) wasn't reachable from the build environment, so this uses a synthetic cohort generated from a logistic model calibrated to published readmission-risk literature — realistic relationships, but not real patients. Disclosed here rather than hidden.

Correlational, not causal

The follow-up effect is adjusted for observed confounders but not randomized. Patients who get a follow-up scheduled may differ in unmeasured ways (health literacy, family support). A true causal claim needs a randomized or quasi-experimental design.

Modest explanatory power

Pseudo-R² of 0.066 and AUC of 0.68 mean the model captures real but partial signal — appropriate for prioritizing outreach, not for denying care or individual clinical decisions.

One cost assumption

The $15,200 figure is a published national average, not this (synthetic) hospital's actual case-mix-adjusted cost per readmission.

With more time or real data, next steps would be

  • Re-run this identical pipeline against a real de-identified dataset under a data use agreement.
  • Add a temporal holdout and calibration curves, not just AUC, before any deployment claim.
  • Test the follow-up effect with a quasi-experimental design (e.g. difference-in-differences around a scheduling policy change) to strengthen the causal claim.
  • Build a small interactive tool so care coordinators could score a patient at the point of discharge.
